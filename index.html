Abstract

Content teams often have to handle a large number of pages and decide which pages should be prioritized. This capstone explores whether measurable signals of search and engagement performance may be used to help prioritize content review. The study uses anonymized data from the FlyRank internship warehouse to develop a decision-support approach based on Random Forest to identify observations that may be worth content review or refresh.

The analysis is based on measurable signals from Google Search Console and Google Analytics 4 such as impressions, average search position, pageviews, sessions, users, organic sessions and scroll events. The modeling target is consistent with the Week-5 approach where an observation is classified as having at least one click. To avoid leakage risk, target-derived variables are excluded from the feature set.

Two validation setups are reported: a random row split and a client-grouped split. The recorded validation results show much stronger performance under the random split, with an R² of 0.7629, compared with 0.1351 under the client-grouped split. The client-grouped outcome is considered the more conservative estimate because observations belonging to the same client are kept on one side of the train/test boundary.

The resulting model output is used as a prioritization aid, not as a prediction of Google's search-ranking algorithm. The results show that validation design has a substantial effect on measured model performance and that machine-learning outputs can support directional decision-making when combined with appropriate limitations and human review.

1. Introduction / Problem Statement

Search-driven content programs may have thousands of pages with a range of visibility and engagement. The problem for content teams is not simply whether a page receives traffic, but which pages should receive attention first.

FlyRank's March 2026 research examined content performance using measurable search and engagement signals, including factors such as content age, freshness, position, traffic mix, content depth and AI visibility. The research also presented machine-learning analyses as exploratory rather than substitutes for direct evidence of performance.

Research Question: Which content pages have measurable performance signals that make them reasonable candidates for content review or refresh?

The capstone addresses this question from a limited decision-support perspective. Instead of attempting to replicate Google's search-ranking system, the study uses measurable signals in the available FlyRank internship dataset to rank observations for human review.

The resulting ranking is not intended to definitively identify a poor page, and a high model score does not guarantee that a content refresh will improve search performance. Search performance is affected by many factors that cannot be fully represented by a single model.

3. Methodology

3.1 Model Approach

The capstone builds on the Week-5 Random Forest classification model used to identify observations that may deserve content review. The Random Forest is treated as a decision-support model for prioritization, not as a reproduction of Google's search-ranking algorithm.

3.2 Features

Seven measurable search and engagement signals are used:

gsc_impressions
gsc_avg_position
ga4_pageviews
ga4_sessions
ga4_users
sessions_organic
scroll_events

3.3 Target Definition

The target follows the Week-5 modeling approach:

1 = page received at least one click
0 = page received no clicks

This target is a modeling construct for content-review prioritization. It should not be interpreted as a direct measure of Google ranking quality.

3.4 Baseline

The Week-4 baseline uses a simple no-action rule: predict that every page does not require action. The baseline is intentionally simple and provides a reference point for the modeling work.

The available capstone output does not provide a separate numerical set of baseline MAE, RMSE, or R² values. Therefore, this paper does not claim a numerical model-versus-baseline improvement that is not directly recorded in the available results.

3.5 Validation Strategy

Two validation designs are retained:

  • Random split: observations are divided into training and testing data using a random row split.
  • Client-grouped split: observations belonging to the same client are kept on only one side of the train/test boundary.

The client-grouped design is treated as the more cautious estimate when discussing potential generalization to unseen clients.

5. Limitations & Honest Framing

This is a decision-support analysis. The model does not reproduce Google's search-ranking algorithm, establish causation, or guarantee that a content refresh will improve search performance.

Dataset scope

The study uses anonymized internship data and therefore represents the available dataset rather than the full population of websites or content programs.

Validation limitation

The large difference between random and client-grouped validation results shows that measured performance depends on validation design.

Target limitation

The target is a modeling construct based on whether an observation received at least one click. It is not a complete measure of content quality or search-ranking quality.

Generalization limitation

The client-grouped result is substantially more conservative, so performance on new clients or new datasets should not be assumed from the random split.

Decision-support limitation

A high model score indicates a review priority under the model. It does not determine what action an analyst should take.

6. Ranked Recommendations

Based on the capstone evidence, the following workflow is recommended.

  1. Use the model to create an initial review queue. The Random Forest ranking can select observations for initial review.
  2. Validate high-priority observations with raw performance data. Before deciding whether a page actually needs action, review impressions, search position, engagement and other available performance signals.
  3. Prioritize visible pages over pages with no measurable demand. FlyRank's broader research suggests that pages with existing visibility can provide more measurable optimization opportunities than pages with little or no demand.
  4. Consider freshness and content quality separately. A model score should not be treated as a substitute for evaluating content freshness, quality, depth or other content considerations.
  5. Maintain human review. The model should indicate where to look; the analyst should decide what, if anything, should be done.
  6. Revalidate on new data. Because the client-grouped result is much more conservative than the random split, future implementations should test the model on new clients or new data before relying on the ranking operationally.
